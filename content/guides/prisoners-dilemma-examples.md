The prisoner's dilemma is the most famous idea in game theory, and it explains a surprising amount of everyday life: why companies start price wars, why athletes dope, and why nobody tidies the office kitchen.

## The original story

Two suspects are arrested and held in separate rooms. The police offer each of them the same deal:

- If you **confess** and your partner stays silent, you go free and they get 10 years.
- If you **both confess**, you each get 5 years.
- If you **both stay silent**, you each get 1 year on a lesser charge.

Look at it from one suspect's point of view. If your partner stays silent, confessing gets you freedom instead of a year. If your partner confesses, confessing gets you 5 years instead of 10. Whatever they do, confessing is better for you.

Your partner reasons the same way. So both confess and both get 5 years, when both staying silent would have got them 1 year each.

That's the dilemma: **each person's best move leads to an outcome that's worse for both.** In game theory terms, confessing is a [dominant strategy](/guides/dominant-strategy-explained), and both confessing is the [Nash equilibrium](/guides/nash-equilibrium-explained).

The game was first framed in 1950 by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation, and the mathematician Albert Tucker gave it the prison story that made it famous.

## 9 real-life prisoner's dilemmas

1. **Price wars.** Two petrol stations on the same road would both earn more at high prices. But each is tempted to undercut and steal the other's customers, so both end up cutting.
2. **Advertising.** If two rival brands both cut their ad budgets, they keep their market shares and save money. Each is tempted to keep spending to grab share, so both keep spending.
3. **Doping in sport.** Every athlete would be better off if nobody doped. But if you think others are doing it, doping looks like the only way to compete.
4. **Arms races.** Two countries would both be safer and richer with fewer weapons, yet each builds more in case the other does.
5. **Overfishing.** Every boat takes a little more, and the stock collapses. This is the [tragedy of the commons](/guides/tragedy-of-the-commons), a many-player version of the same game.
6. **Group projects.** Everyone hopes someone else will do the work, so the work doesn't get done.
7. **Splitting the bill.** When a restaurant bill is split equally, ordering the expensive dish costs you only a fraction of its price, so everyone orders more. Economists call it the [diner's dilemma](/guides/splitting-the-bill-game-theory).
8. **Climate action.** Each country gains if all cut emissions, but each is tempted to let others bear the cost.
9. **Golden Balls.** The UK game show's "split or steal" final is a televised prisoner's dilemma, and it produced some of TV's most famous moments.

## How people escape it

If the prisoner's dilemma always ended badly, cooperation would be rare. It isn't. People escape the trap in four main ways.

- **Repetition.** When you'll play again, cheating today costs you tomorrow. Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments around 1980 found that a simple strategy, [tit for tat](/guides/tit-for-tat-strategy), did very well when the game repeated.
- **Enforceable promises.** Contracts, deposits and regulators turn "I'll cooperate" from cheap talk into a commitment.
- **Changing the payoffs.** Fines for doping, quotas for fishing and bonuses for team results all make cooperation the better individual choice.
- **Trust and reputation.** People who'll deal with each other again, or whose reputations are public, behave better.

## Why it matters

Once you can spot a prisoner's dilemma, you stop asking "why are these people being so stupid?" and start asking "what would make cooperation pay?" That's usually the more useful question, whether you're running a team, a business or a negotiation.
