Your power in a negotiation doesn't come from how hard you push. It comes from what you'll do if the talks fail. Negotiators have a name for that: your **BATNA**, or best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

The term comes from Roger Fisher and William Ury's 1981 book *Getting to Yes*, written out of the Harvard Negotiation Project. It's one of the most useful ideas in negotiation because it turns a vague feeling ("I think I'm in a strong position") into something concrete you can check and improve.

## BATNA, in one example

Say you've been offered a job at £40,000 and you'd like £45,000.

- **No other offer:** if the talks fail, you stay where you are, or keep job hunting. Pushing feels risky, and the other side can usually sense it.
- **A second offer at £43,000:** now your worst case is £43,000, not nothing. Asking for £45,000 is easy, and you can say so calmly.

Same person, same skills, same employer. The only thing that changed is the alternative. That alternative is your BATNA.

## BATNA is not your bottom line

People mix these up, but they're different things:

- **Your BATNA** is an action you can actually take: another offer, another supplier, another buyer, or simply doing nothing.
- **Your reservation price** is the worst deal you'd accept. It's a number, and it should come from your BATNA. If your alternative is worth £43,000, a deal below that makes you worse off. (More on this in our guide to the [reservation price](/guides/reservation-price).)

The gap between your reservation price and theirs is the [ZOPA, the zone of possible agreement](/guides/zopa-negotiation). Every deal lands somewhere inside it.

## Work out their BATNA too

Most people stop at their own. The better question is what happens to *them* if there's no deal.

A landlord with an empty flat in January, a supplier with spare capacity at quarter end, or a hiring manager who's already lost two candidates all have weak alternatives. You have more power than you think. Equally, if the other side has five people queuing behind you, a hard line won't work however confident you sound.

Ask yourself:

1. What will they do if we don't agree?
2. How quickly do they need a deal?
3. What would replacing me cost them in time and money?

## Four ways to improve your BATNA

The best time to improve your alternative is before you sit down, not halfway through the meeting.

1. **Create a real second option.** Get the other quote, apply for the other job, view the other flat. Even one credible alternative changes how you negotiate.
2. **Make doing nothing more bearable.** If you can comfortably stay in your current job or keep your current car another year, walking away stops being scary.
3. **Find out the real numbers.** A vague alternative is weak. "I could probably get more elsewhere" is cheap talk. "Two agencies quoted £1,850" is a position.
4. **Buy time.** Deadlines weaken alternatives. Starting early gives you room to say no.

## When to reveal it

If your BATNA is strong and believable, let them know, politely. "I have another offer at £43,000, but I'd rather work here" is persuasive because it's specific and it isn't a threat.

If it's weak, don't bluff. A bluff that gets called leaves you worse off than saying nothing. Keep the conversation on value and on what both sides need.

## The takeaway

Before your next negotiation, write down one line: *If this fails, I will...* If you can't finish the sentence, your first job isn't to negotiate harder. It's to build a better alternative.
