One question shapes almost every negotiation: is this a fixed pie, or can it grow? Game theorists put it more formally. Is the game **zero-sum** or **positive-sum**?

## Zero-sum: my gain is your loss

In a zero-sum game, the total is fixed. Every pound I win, you lose. Poker, chess and splitting a single bill are zero-sum.

The strategy here is about claiming value: [anchoring](/guides/anchoring-in-negotiation), holding your ground and knowing your [walk-away point](/guides/reservation-price).

## Positive-sum: we can both win

In a positive-sum game, the total can grow. A trade only happens because each side values what they get more than what they give. You value the coffee more than £3.50; the café values the £3.50 more than the coffee. Both walk away better off.

Most business, most jobs and most long-term relationships are positive-sum.

## Negative-sum: we both lose

Some games shrink the pie. Price wars, legal battles and strikes often cost both sides more than either gains. The [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples) is a classic case where self-interest pushes both players into a negative outcome.

## Why the difference matters

The costliest mistake in negotiation is treating a positive-sum game as zero-sum. If you think every concession is a loss, you'll fight over price and miss the trades that would make both sides better off.

Take a job offer. Salary looks zero-sum: every extra pound for you costs the employer a pound. But start date, remote days, training budget and job title often cost the employer little and matter a lot to you. Add them to the table and the pie grows. Negotiators call this [integrative negotiation](/guides/integrative-vs-distributive-negotiation).

## How to turn a zero-sum fight into a positive-sum deal

1. **Ask why, not just what.** "Why does that deadline matter?" often reveals interests you can meet cheaply.
2. **Add issues.** Price, timing, quality, risk and payment terms all have different values to each side.
3. **Trade, don't split.** Give ground on what you care about less in exchange for what you care about more.
4. **Then claim your share.** Positive-sum doesn't mean soft. Once you've grown the pie, you still need to claim a fair slice.

The best negotiators do both: they grow the pie, then they divide it well.
