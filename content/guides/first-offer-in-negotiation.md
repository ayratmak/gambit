Conventional wisdom says never name a number first. The research says it's more complicated, and often the opposite.

## The case for going first

A first offer works as an [anchor](/guides/anchoring-in-negotiation): it pulls the final deal towards it. In a 2001 study, Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler found that whoever made the first offer tended to get a better outcome, and that first offers were strongly linked to final prices.

Going first also lets you frame the conversation. Instead of reacting to their number, they're reacting to yours.

## The case for waiting

Going first is risky when you know little about the value. If you're selling something you've never priced, or applying for a job without knowing the band, your first offer might be well below what they'd have paid.

Letting them go first can reveal information, and sometimes a pleasant surprise.

## A simple rule

- **Know the value? Go first, and aim high but defensible.**
- **Don't know the value? Let them go first, or ask questions until you do.**

Most of the time, the fix is preparation. If you research comparable prices, salaries or quotes, you'll usually know enough to open.

## How to make a strong first offer

1. **Aim at the edge of what you can justify**, not at your target. You need room to concede.
2. **Give your reason.** "Based on three comparable sales at £312,000 to £325,000..." makes the number feel earned.
3. **Use a precise figure.** Precise numbers tend to draw smaller counteroffers than round ones.
4. **Stop talking.** Don't undercut yourself by adding "but I'm flexible".

## If they go first

- **Don't react to an extreme number with a counter.** Ask how they got there.
- **Re-anchor.** Put your own reasoned figure on the table and talk about that.
- **Remember the [ZOPA](/guides/zopa-negotiation).** Their opening isn't their limit, any more than yours is.

The first number sets the frame for everything that follows. Whoever prepares best usually gets to set it.
