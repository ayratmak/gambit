Around 1980, the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited experts to a strange competition. Each sent in a computer program to play the [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples) against every other program, over and over. Points for cooperating, more points for cheating a cooperator, and nothing good for mutual cheating.

The entries included sophisticated programs built to exploit, deceive and outguess the opposition. The winner was the shortest program submitted.

## The strategy

It was called **tit for tat**, sent in by the psychologist Anatol Rapoport, and it had just two rules:

1. Cooperate on the first move.
2. After that, do whatever the other player did last time.

Axelrod ran a second, larger tournament. Entrants knew tit for tat had won and tried to beat it. It won again.

## Why it works

Axelrod picked out four qualities, and they're useful well beyond computer games:

- **It's nice.** It never cheats first, so it doesn't start fights.
- **It retaliates.** If you cheat it, it cheats back straight away, so exploiting it doesn't pay.
- **It forgives.** As soon as you cooperate again, it does too. One bad move doesn't start a war that never ends.
- **It's clear.** Its behaviour is so simple that opponents quickly learn cooperating is the best response.

Notice that tit for tat never beats the player in front of it. In any single match it can only draw or lose narrowly. It wins the tournament because it brings out cooperation in others, so it piles up points over many relationships.

## The weakness

Tit for tat has one flaw: mistakes. If one move is misread as cheating, two tit for tat players can get stuck retaliating against each other forever. In the real world, where emails are misread and deliveries are late, more forgiving versions do better, for example ones that occasionally let a defection go.

## Using it in negotiation and work

The four rules make a surprisingly good guide to long-term relationships with suppliers, colleagues and clients:

1. **Open cooperatively.** Share information and make a reasonable first move.
2. **Respond to bad behaviour, promptly and proportionately.** If a supplier misses a deadline without warning, say so straight away rather than letting it slide.
3. **Don't hold grudges.** When they fix it, go back to cooperating.
4. **Be predictable.** People cooperate more when they can see what you'll do.

It won't win every round. It's built to win the relationship.
