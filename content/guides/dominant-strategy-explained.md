Most decisions are hard because the best move depends on what others will do. Occasionally you get lucky: one option beats all the others **whatever anyone else does.** Game theorists call that a **dominant strategy**, and spotting one makes a decision easy.

## A simple example

Two coffee shops are deciding whether to offer free Wi-Fi. Customers like it, so:

- If your rival offers Wi-Fi and you don't, you lose customers.
- If your rival doesn't and you do, you win customers.

Offering Wi-Fi is better for you in both cases. It's a dominant strategy. You don't need to guess what your rival will do.

## How to spot one in a payoff grid

Draw your options down the side and the other player's along the top. Then compare your payoffs row by row:

1. Pick one of your options.
2. Check it against each column. Is it better than every other option you have, in every column?
3. If yes, it's dominant.

If one of your options is always *worse* than another, it's dominated, and you can cross it off. Crossing off dominated options often turns a complicated game into a simple one.

## When the dominant move hurts everyone

Here's the twist. In the [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples), confessing is a dominant strategy for both players. Each is better off confessing whatever the other does. Yet when both follow their dominant strategy, both end up worse off than if they'd both stayed silent.

The same thing happens in advertising wars, price cuts and arms races. Each side's dominant move adds up to a bad result for everyone. That's why the fix is usually to change the game, with contracts, rules or repeated dealings, rather than to hope people act against their own interest.

## Dominant strategies in real life

- **Sealed-bid second-price auctions.** In an auction where the winner pays the second-highest bid, bidding your true value is a dominant strategy. That's why eBay-style proxy bidding asks for your maximum. (See our guide to [auction theory](/guides/auction-theory-explained).)
- **Turning up prepared.** In negotiation, preparing your alternatives and numbers helps you whatever the other side does.
- **Insurance against rare disasters.** If the cost is small and the downside huge, buying cover can dominate going without.

## Why it matters

When you face a decision involving other people, start by asking: is there an option that's best whatever they do? If there is, take it. If there isn't, you need to think about what they'll do, and that's where [Nash equilibrium](/guides/nash-equilibrium-explained) and the rest of game theory come in.
