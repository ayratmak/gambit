Some people interrupt, threaten, stonewall or move the goalposts. The instinct is to fight back or give in. Neither works well. Here are seven techniques that do.

## 1. Don't react straight away

When someone gets aggressive, your first instinct is the one they're counting on. William Ury, in *Getting Past No* (1991), calls the antidote "going to the balcony": mentally stepping back to watch the conversation before you reply. A pause, a sip of water or "let me think about that" is enough.

## 2. Name what's happening

Labelling emotions, a technique popularised by former FBI negotiator [Chris Voss](/guides/chris-voss-negotiation-techniques), takes the heat out of a conversation:

> "It sounds like you're under real pressure to get this signed before the end of the quarter."

People calm down when they feel understood, even by someone they're negotiating against.

## 3. Separate the person from the problem

Attack the issue, not them. "This timeline doesn't work for us" lands very differently from "You're being unreasonable."

## 4. Ask questions instead of arguing

Questions give the other side something to do other than defend:

- "What would need to happen for this to work for you?"
- "How did you arrive at that figure?"
- "What's the concern behind that?"

## 5. Use objective criteria

When positions are dug in, move to standards neither side controls: market rates, published prices, expert valuations, precedent. It's much easier for someone to accept a fair standard than to back down to you.

## 6. Build them a golden bridge

Stubborn people often need a way to say yes without losing face. Offer them a story they can tell their boss: "We've agreed a phased price increase linked to inflation" sounds better than "We gave in."

## 7. Know your exit

If nothing works, check your [alternative](/guides/what-is-batna). If it's better than any deal on the table, a calm "I don't think we can make this work, so let's leave it here" is often the moment the other side becomes reasonable. (See [when to walk away](/guides/when-to-walk-away).)

## What not to do

- **Match their aggression.** It escalates and makes agreement harder.
- **Make concessions to buy peace.** It teaches them that pressure works.
- **Take it personally.** Difficult behaviour is usually a tactic or a sign of pressure, not a judgement on you.
