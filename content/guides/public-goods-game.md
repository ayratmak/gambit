Everyone uses the office milk. Nobody wants to buy it. Economists have a name for this situation, and a famous experiment that explains why it happens and how to fix it.

## The game

Four players each get £20. Each decides how much to put into a shared pot. Whatever goes into the pot is doubled and split equally among all four, whatever they contributed.

- If **everyone puts in £20**, the pot is £80, doubled to £160, so everyone gets £40. Everyone doubles their money.
- If **you put in nothing** while the others put in £20, the pot is £60, doubled to £120, so everyone gets £30. You keep your £20 too, so you finish with £50.

Whatever others do, keeping your money pays you more. But if everyone thinks that way, nobody contributes and everyone ends with just £20 instead of £40. It's the [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples) with a crowd.

## What people actually do

In laboratory versions, people are more generous than pure self-interest predicts. They typically start by putting in around half their money. But round after round, as players notice others contributing less, they cut back too. Contributions slide towards almost nothing.

Most people aren't selfish. Many are **conditional cooperators**: happy to chip in as long as others do. Free riders don't just take for themselves; they slowly teach everyone else to stop contributing.

## The punishment twist

In 2000, the economists Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter ran a version where, after each round, players could pay a little of their own money to reduce a free rider's earnings. Punishing cost the punisher money and brought no direct gain.

People punished anyway. And contributions shot back up and stayed high. The mere chance of being called out was enough to keep cooperation going.

## Lessons for the office (and beyond)

1. **Make contributions visible.** Free riding thrives in the dark.
2. **Agree a simple norm.** "Whoever finishes the milk buys the next one."
3. **Let people respond.** It doesn't need to be a fine. A friendly "your turn for the milk" works.
4. **Don't rely on goodwill alone.** Conditional cooperators need to see that others are cooperating.

So the milk doesn't need a rota. It needs someone willing to say something. (For the big-picture version of this problem, see the [tragedy of the commons](/guides/tragedy-of-the-commons).)
