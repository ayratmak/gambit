It's December. Ten people, one long table, one bill. Someone says "let's just split it". Here's what game theory says will happen next.

## The logic

Say four friends split the bill equally. You're deciding between a £14 pasta and a £40 steak.

- If you **pay for your own food**, the steak costs you £26 more.
- If you **split four ways**, the steak adds £26 to the bill but only £6.50 to your share. Your friends pay the other £19.50.

Suddenly the steak looks like a bargain. But everyone at the table does the same maths. So everyone upgrades, and everyone ends up paying more than they would have if they'd ordered for themselves.

That's the **diner's dilemma**: a restaurant-sized [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples).

## The evidence

In 2004, the economists Uri Gneezy, Ernan Haruvy and Hadas Yafe tested this with real diners at a university restaurant in Israel. Groups who split the bill equally ordered noticeably more than groups who paid individually. In another condition where the meal was free, people ordered the most of all.

## Why it matters beyond dinner

The same logic appears whenever costs are shared but choices are individual:

- **Office stationery and kitchens**, where nobody pays directly. (See the [public goods game](/guides/public-goods-game).)
- **All-you-can-eat buffets.**
- **Shared company budgets**, where each team spends as if it's someone else's money.
- **Insurance**, where being covered can make people less careful.

## Fairer ways to pay

- **Pay for what you ordered.** Most payment apps make splitting by item easy.
- **Split only shared dishes and drinks**, and pay for mains individually.
- **Agree a budget per head in advance** for big group meals.
- **If you do split equally, say so before ordering**, so nobody feels exploited later.

So, at your next Christmas meal: split it, or pay your own?
