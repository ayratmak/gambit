Game theory is the study of decisions where **your best move depends on what other people do, and theirs depends on you.** That describes most of life. Here are twelve everyday examples, each with the idea behind it.

## 1. Ice cream vans on a beach

Two vans on a long beach end up parked side by side in the middle, even though spreading out would serve customers better. Each moves towards the centre to grab more of the beach. Economists call this Hotelling's model, and it also explains why rival shops cluster and why political parties chase the centre.

## 2. Penalty kicks

A goalkeeper who always dives the same way gets beaten. So do penalty takers who always shoot the same way. The best strategy is to be unpredictable, which game theorists call a [mixed strategy](/guides/mixed-strategy-penalty-kicks).

## 3. Price wars

Two petrol stations would both earn more at high prices, but each is tempted to undercut the other. That's a [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples).

## 4. Splitting the bill

When a dinner bill is split equally, the extra cost of your expensive dish is shared, so everyone orders more. It's known as the [diner's dilemma](/guides/splitting-the-bill-game-theory).

## 5. The office kitchen

Everyone uses the milk, nobody wants to buy it. That's a [public goods game](/guides/public-goods-game), and it explains a lot about shared resources.

## 6. Driving on the left

Once everyone drives on one side, nobody wants to switch. That's a [Nash equilibrium](/guides/nash-equilibrium-explained), and also a coordination game: the right-hand side works just as well, as long as everyone agrees.

## 7. Meeting a friend without a plan

If you lose a friend in a city with no phone, you'll both head somewhere obvious, like the main station at noon. Thomas Schelling called these [focal points](/guides/schelling-focal-point).

## 8. Auctions

Winning an auction can mean you overpaid, because the winner is often the person who overestimated the value most. That's the [winner's curse](/guides/winners-curse).

## 9. Bank runs

If you think others will withdraw their money, withdrawing yours first is rational, even if the bank was fine. The fear itself causes the crisis.

## 10. Peacocks' tails

A huge tail is a handicap, which is exactly why it's a believable signal of fitness. Biologists call this the handicap principle, and it's a classic example of [costly signalling](/guides/signalling-game-theory).

## 11. Two people heading for each other

Two drivers on a narrow road both want the other to give way. That's the [game of chicken](/guides/game-of-chicken), and the winner is often whoever commits first.

## 12. Group projects

If you suspect nobody else will try, doing the minimum is the safe play. That's the [stag hunt](/guides/stag-hunt), a game about trust.

## Why it's worth learning

None of these needs maths to understand. What they share is a way of thinking: before you decide, ask what the other side will do, and what they think you'll do. That habit alone makes you a better negotiator, manager and decision-maker.
