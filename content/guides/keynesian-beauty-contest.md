Here's a game to play at the Christmas table. Everyone writes down a whole number from 0 to 100. The winner is whoever is closest to **two-thirds of the average** of all the numbers.

What would you pick? Decide before reading on.

## The logic

- If people picked at random, the average would be about 50, and two-thirds of that is about 33.
- But if everyone thinks that, they'll all pick 33, so the target becomes 22.
- If everyone thinks *that*, the target becomes about 15, then 10, then 7...

Keep going and you reach 0. If everyone is perfectly logical and knows everyone else is too, everyone picks 0. That's the [Nash equilibrium](/guides/nash-equilibrium-explained).

## What people actually pick

In practice, 0 almost never wins. When the game has been run in classrooms and newspapers, winning numbers have often landed in the high teens to low 30s. In 1997 the *Financial Times* ran a version with prize money, and the winning number was 13. Most people stop after one or two steps of reasoning, and a few pick wildly high numbers.

The economist Rosemarie Nagel's experiments in the 1990s made this famous. They showed that people think in levels: some don't think about others at all, many think one step ahead, fewer think two steps, and very few go all the way.

## Where the name comes from

In 1936, John Maynard Keynes compared investing to a newspaper beauty contest where readers won by choosing the faces *other readers* would choose. You don't pick what you think is prettiest. You pick what you think others think is prettiest, or what others think others think.

That's why markets sometimes move on expectations about expectations, rather than on facts.

## The lesson

**The right answer depends on how far ahead the other players think.** Being too clever can lose as surely as not thinking at all. In negotiation, business and markets, the skill is to think one step further than the people you're actually dealing with, not one step further than a perfect robot.

So, what did you pick?
