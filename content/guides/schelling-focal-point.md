You have to meet a stranger in New York tomorrow. You can't contact each other. Where do you go, and when?

The economist Thomas Schelling asked his students exactly this. Many picked the same place, Grand Central Station, and almost all picked noon. They coordinated without talking, because each expected the other to pick the obvious answer.

Schelling called these answers **focal points**. He wrote about them in his 1960 book *The Strategy of Conflict*, and the idea helped earn him a share of the 2005 Nobel prize in economics.

## Why focal points work

Many situations have several possible solutions that would all work, as long as everyone picks the same one. These are coordination games. With no way to talk, people look for the option that stands out: the most famous landmark, the round number, the halfway point, the first on the list.

It's not that the focal point is best. It's that everyone expects everyone else to see it.

## Focal points in everyday life

- **Meeting places.** "Under the clock at the station."
- **Tipping.** Round percentages like 10% or 12.5% feel natural.
- **Prices.** £9.99 and £10 are focal; £10.37 is not.
- **Borders.** Rivers, mountain ridges and straight lines of latitude become borders partly because they're obvious.

## Focal points in negotiation

Deals tend to settle on focal points too: round numbers, previous year's price, the market rate, or splitting the difference.

That last one deserves care. **Fifty-fifty feels fair because it's obvious, not because it is fair.** The midpoint depends on where each side started. If the other side opened with an extreme number, meeting in the middle hands them a win.

## How to use focal points

1. **Shape the midpoint.** A well-reasoned [first offer](/guides/first-offer-in-negotiation) moves the middle towards you.
2. **Check where the middle is** before you agree to split the difference.
3. **Offer a principled focal point.** "Let's use the published market rate" or "last year's price plus inflation" gives both sides an obvious place to land.
4. **Be wary of round numbers that suit them.** A neat £50,000 might feel natural, but the right figure might be £53,500.
