Every boat takes a few more fish. Every walker cuts the same corner across the grass. Every office worker uses the last of the milk. Each choice makes sense on its own, and together they ruin the shared thing everyone relies on. That's the **tragedy of the commons**.

## Where the idea comes from

In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin published an essay in *Science* called "The Tragedy of the Commons". He borrowed an example from the economist William Forster Lloyd, writing in 1833: herders sharing a common pasture.

Each herder gains the full benefit of adding one more cow, but the cost, slightly less grass, is shared by everyone. So each adds another cow, and another, until the pasture is grazed bare.

## The game behind it

It's a many-player [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples). For each person, taking more is the better individual choice whatever others do. When everyone follows that logic, everyone ends up worse off.

## Real examples

- **Fishing.** Off Newfoundland, cod stocks collapsed so badly that Canada closed the northern cod fishery in 1992, putting tens of thousands of people out of work.
- **Traffic.** Each extra car adds a little congestion for everyone else.
- **Antibiotics.** Each unnecessary prescription adds a little to resistance that affects everyone.
- **The office kitchen.** Everyone uses the milk; nobody wants to buy it. (See the [public goods game](/guides/public-goods-game).)
- **Group chats and shared inboxes.** Everyone's small message adds to everyone's overload.

## How communities avoid it

Hardin thought the only fixes were government control or private ownership. The political economist Elinor Ostrom showed there was a third way. Studying fisheries, forests and irrigation systems around the world, she found many communities managing shared resources well for centuries. For that work she became the first woman to win the Nobel prize in economics, in 2009.

The successful communities tended to have:

1. **Clear boundaries** on who can use the resource.
2. **Rules that fit local conditions**, made by the people affected.
3. **Monitoring** by users or people accountable to them.
4. **Graduated sanctions**: small penalties first, bigger ones for repeat offenders.
5. **Cheap ways to settle disputes.**

## The takeaway

When something shared keeps getting used up, blaming people rarely helps. Change the game: make usage visible, agree simple rules, and make sure someone notices when they're broken.
