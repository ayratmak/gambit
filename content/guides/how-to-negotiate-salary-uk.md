Negotiating your salary is uncomfortable, and that's exactly why it pays. A few minutes of polite conversation can be worth thousands of pounds a year, and because future rises are usually a percentage of your current pay, the gap compounds for years.

Here's a simple process that works for most UK job offers.

## 1. Do your homework before the offer

You can't negotiate well from a feeling. Before the offer arrives, gather:

- **Market rates** for the role, level and city, from job adverts that show salaries, recruiters and published salary surveys.
- **Your alternatives.** Other interviews, other offers, or staying put. This is your [BATNA](/guides/what-is-batna), and it's where your confidence comes from.
- **Your number.** The figure you'd love, and the lowest you'd accept. Write both down.

## 2. Don't name your number too early

Recruiters often ask "what are you on now?" or "what are you looking for?" in the first call. You don't have to answer with a single figure.

> "I'd like to learn more about the role first. What's the budgeted range?"

If they press, give a range anchored on your target, not your minimum. A range like "£48,000 to £52,000" tells them the bottom is £48,000, so make sure that bottom is still a good outcome for you. The [anchoring](/guides/anchoring-in-negotiation) effect is strong, so the first number matters.

## 3. Thank them, then pause

When the offer comes, don't accept on the call, however good it sounds.

> "Thank you, I'm really pleased. Can I take a day or two to look at the whole package?"

This isn't rude. It's normal, and it gives you time to plan your counter.

## 4. Make one clear, reasoned counter

Ask for a specific number and give one or two reasons that matter to them, not to you. Your rent going up is your problem. What you'll deliver in the role is theirs.

> "I'm excited about the role. Based on the scope and what similar roles are paying, I was hoping for £52,000. If we can get there, I'm ready to sign this week."

Precise, well-justified numbers tend to get less pushback than round ones, because they sound researched. If you prefer email, our guide on [how to counter a job offer by email](/guides/how-to-counter-a-job-offer-email) has templates.

## 5. If the salary is fixed, trade other things

"The budget is fixed" is often true for base pay and false for everything else. Things that are cheap for the employer but valuable to you include:

- a later or earlier start date
- an extra remote day
- more holiday
- a signing bonus
- a training or conference budget
- an earlier first pay review, for example at six months

Negotiators call this [logrolling](/guides/integrative-vs-distributive-negotiation): trading things each side values differently.

## 6. Get it in writing

Once you agree, ask for the revised offer in writing before you resign from your current job.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Accepting on the spot.** You lose your only moment of leverage.
- **Apologising.** "Sorry, I know this is cheeky, but..." invites a no.
- **Bluffing about other offers.** If you're found out, trust is gone.
- **Negotiating against yourself.** Ask once, then wait. Don't lower your number before they've replied.
- **Making it about need instead of value.** Employers pay for what you'll do, not for your costs.

## The bottom line

Most employers expect a counter-offer, and very few withdraw an offer over a polite, reasoned request. The worst common outcome is "this is the best we can do", which leaves you exactly where you started.
