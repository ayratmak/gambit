January is the most common time for freelancers and consultants to raise their rates. Clients are setting budgets, and "new year, new rates" feels natural. Here's how to do it without losing the clients you want to keep.

## Signs it's time

- **You're fully booked**, or turning work away.
- **Clients say yes instantly** to your quotes, without pushback.
- **Your skills have grown** since you set your rate.
- **Your costs have risen** and your margin has shrunk.
- **It's been more than a year** since your last increase.

If several of these apply, you're probably undercharging.

## Start with new clients

The easiest place to raise rates is with new clients, who've never seen your old price. Quote the new rate confidently, with no apology. Use a specific number with a reason. (See [anchoring](/guides/anchoring-in-negotiation).)

## Give existing clients notice

For clients you already work with, give clear notice, typically four to eight weeks, and frame it around the value you deliver.

> Subject: My rates for 2027
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I've really enjoyed working together this year, particularly on [project or result].
>
> From 1 March, my day rate will be £[new rate]. It reflects the growth in what I bring to projects like ours, and it's the first change since [date].
>
> All work booked before 1 March will stay at the current rate. Happy to talk it through if useful.
>
> Best wishes,
> [Your name]

## Handle pushback with options

If a client can't stretch, don't just cave. Offer choices:

- **A smaller scope** at the new rate.
- **A retainer** with a small discount for committed volume.
- **A phased increase**, with part now and the rest in six months.

This is [logrolling](/guides/integrative-vs-distributive-negotiation): giving on terms that matter less to you.

## Know your alternative

Your confidence comes from your [BATNA](/guides/what-is-batna). If you have a pipeline of other work, losing one low-paying client may free up time for a better one. If you don't, start building one before you raise rates.

## Accept some attrition

If nobody ever says no, your rates are still too low. Losing your lowest-paying client is often the fastest way to make room for higher-paying work. (See [when to walk away](/guides/when-to-walk-away).)
