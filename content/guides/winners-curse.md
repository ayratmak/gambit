You won the auction. Congratulations. Now ask yourself the uncomfortable question: why did nobody else bid this much?

## Where the idea comes from

In 1971, three petroleum engineers at Atlantic Richfield, Ed Capen, Bob Clapp and William Campbell, published a paper about oil companies bidding for offshore drilling leases in the Gulf of Mexico. Companies kept winning leases and then earning poor returns. Their explanation became known as the **winner's curse**.

## How it works

Imagine a jar of coins. Everyone in a room estimates its value and bids. The coins are worth the same to everyone; people just guess differently. Some guess too low, some too high, and the average guess might be about right.

But who wins? The person with the *highest* guess. So the winner is usually someone who overestimated, and they pay more than the jar is worth.

That's the curse: **in common-value auctions, winning is evidence that you overestimated.**

## Where it shows up

- **Oil and mining rights**, where nobody knows exactly what's underground.
- **Company takeovers**, where the winning bidder may have been the most optimistic about future profits.
- **Football transfers**, where clubs compete for the same player.
- **Bidding wars on houses**, especially in sealed "best and final offers" rounds.
- **Spectrum auctions** for mobile phone licences.

## How to avoid it

1. **Shade your bid.** Bid below your honest estimate. The more bidders and the more uncertain the value, the more you should shade.
2. **Set your limit before you start.** Write it down. (See the [dollar auction](/guides/sunk-cost-dollar-auction) for what happens if you don't.)
3. **Ask what others know.** If experts are dropping out, that's information.
4. **Separate value from wanting.** The thrill of winning is not part of an asset's value.

## The flip side for sellers

If you're selling something whose value is uncertain, auctions where bidders can see others' bids, or that give bidders more information, can reduce their fear of the curse and lead to higher bids. That's one reason auction design matters so much. (More in our guide to [auction theory](/guides/auction-theory-explained).)

Winning feels great. Make sure it's also a good deal.
