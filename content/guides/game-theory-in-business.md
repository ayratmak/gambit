Business is full of decisions where your best move depends on how others respond: rivals, customers, suppliers and regulators. That's exactly what game theory studies. Here are eight lessons managers can use.

## 1. Look forward, reason back

Before you cut a price or launch a product, ask how your rivals will respond, and how you'll respond to that. [Backward induction](/guides/backward-induction) turns a gamble into a plan.

## 2. Beware the price war

Two rivals cutting prices to steal share often end up with the same shares and lower margins. That's a [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples). Competing on service, quality or features can be a better game to play.

## 3. Commitments change the game

A visible, credible commitment, such as building spare capacity or publicly promising never to be undersold, can deter rivals from entering or attacking. The same logic drives the [game of chicken](/guides/game-of-chicken).

## 4. Price matching isn't always what it seems

A promise to match any rival's price can discourage rivals from cutting at all. Economists have argued since the 1980s that such clauses can soften competition. (More in our guide to [Black Friday game theory](/guides/black-friday-game-theory).)

## 5. Signals must be costly to be believed

Anyone can claim quality. Long warranties, money-back guarantees and heavy investment in a brand are believable because they'd be expensive for a weak product. That's [signalling](/guides/signalling-game-theory).

## 6. Auctions need design

How you sell matters as much as what you sell. Governments that design spectrum auctions carefully have raised large sums, while poorly designed ones have disappointed. If you buy at auction, watch out for the [winner's curse](/guides/winners-curse).

## 7. Repeated games reward reputation

With customers and suppliers you'll see again, short-term grabs damage long-term returns. Being fair and predictable, like [tit for tat](/guides/tit-for-tat-strategy), builds trust that pays over many rounds.

## 8. Change the game, not just your move

The best strategists don't only play the game they're given. They change it: adding partners, creating switching costs, bundling products or changing the rules of a tender. Asking "what game are we in, and can we play a better one?" is often the most valuable question in a strategy meeting.

## Start small

You don't need complex models. A simple table of your options against a rival's options, with rough payoffs, is enough to spot dominant strategies, likely responses and traps.
