If you want to get better at negotiating over the holidays, these eight books cover the ideas that matter most. Here's the key lesson from each.

## 1. Getting to Yes, by Roger Fisher and William Ury (1981)

The classic from the Harvard Negotiation Project. It introduced the idea of the [BATNA](/guides/what-is-batna) and the principle of focusing on interests rather than positions.

**Key idea:** separate the people from the problem, and negotiate on interests, not positions.

## 2. Never Split the Difference, by Chris Voss (2016)

A former FBI hostage negotiator's practical toolkit. Highly readable, full of stories.

**Key idea:** make the other side feel understood, using techniques like [mirroring and labelling](/guides/chris-voss-negotiation-techniques).

## 3. Influence, by Robert Cialdini (1984)

The standard book on the psychology of persuasion, built on decades of research.

**Key idea:** people say yes for predictable reasons, such as reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity.

## 4. Getting Past No, by William Ury (1991)

The follow-up to *Getting to Yes*, focused on difficult people and stalled talks.

**Key idea:** step back before reacting, then build the other side a "golden bridge" to agreement. (See [negotiating with difficult people](/guides/negotiating-with-difficult-people).)

## 5. Negotiation Genius, by Deepak Malhotra and Max Bazerman (2007)

From two Harvard Business School professors, combining research with practical frameworks.

**Key idea:** prepare systematically, and look for value others miss.

## 6. Bargaining for Advantage, by G. Richard Shell (first published 1999)

A Wharton professor's guide that takes personality and style seriously.

**Key idea:** know your own style and adapt it to the situation. (See [the 5 negotiation styles](/guides/negotiation-styles).)

## 7. Thinking Strategically, by Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff (1991)

Game theory for everyday decisions and business, with lots of examples and almost no maths.

**Key idea:** look forward and reason back. Anticipate how others will respond before you move.

## 8. The Strategy of Conflict, by Thomas Schelling (1960)

The most academic book here, and one of the most influential. Schelling's ideas on commitment and [focal points](/guides/schelling-focal-point) shaped how we think about bargaining.

**Key idea:** limiting your own options can strengthen your position.

## Reading isn't enough

Every one of these books makes the same point in its own way: negotiation is a skill you build by practising. Pick one technique, try it this week, and notice what happens. If you'd rather learn by playing, our [free course](/free) turns these ideas into games.
