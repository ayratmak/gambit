Chris Voss spent years as the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator, then wrote *Never Split the Difference* (2016), one of the best-selling negotiation books of the last decade. His techniques are popular because they're simple, quick to learn and work in everyday conversations, not just hostage situations.

## 1. Mirroring

**What it is:** Repeat the last one to three words the other person said, as a question. Then stay quiet.

> **Them:** "We can't go above £40,000 for this role."
> **You:** "Above £40,000?"

People tend to fill the silence by explaining, and the explanation often reveals what's really behind the number.

There's research behind the instinct. In a 2003 Dutch study, Rick van Baaren and colleagues found that waitresses who repeated customers' orders back word for word received bigger tips. People like people who sound like them.

## 2. Labelling

**What it is:** Name the emotion or concern you think the other person has. Start with "It sounds like...", "It seems like..." or "It looks like...".

> "It sounds like you've been burned by suppliers missing deadlines before."

A good label shows you understand, lowers the temperature and invites them to correct or expand on it.

## 3. Calibrated questions

**What it is:** Open questions starting with "how" or "what" that invite the other side to solve your problem.

> "How am I supposed to do that on this budget?"
> "What would need to be true for this to work for you?"

They're harder to answer with a flat no than "Can you do better?"

## 4. The accusation audit

**What it is:** Name the worst things the other side might think about you before they say them.

> "You probably think I'm going to ask for the moon and waste your afternoon."

Saying it first takes the sting out and often makes them reassure you.

## 5. Aim for "that's right"

Voss argues the most important moment in a negotiation is when the other person says "that's right", because it means they feel understood. Summarise their position so well that they agree with your summary.

## 6. Don't split the difference

Meeting in the middle sounds fair, but the middle depends on where each side started. If they opened with an extreme number, splitting the difference rewards them. (More on this in our guide to [focal points](/guides/schelling-focal-point).)

## How to start using these

Pick one technique and use it in an ordinary conversation this week. Mirroring is the easiest: three words and some silence. Most people are surprised how much the other person tells them.
