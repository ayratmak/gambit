The first number on the table has a strange power. Even when everyone knows it's just an opening bid, it pulls the final deal towards it. Psychologists call this **anchoring**, and it's one of the most reliable effects in negotiation research.

## Where the idea comes from

In a famous 1974 study, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune in front of participants and then asked them to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. People who'd seen a higher number on the wheel gave higher estimates. A number they knew was random still shaped their answer.

In negotiation, the effect is even stronger, because the first offer isn't random. It comes with a confident person attached.

## Even experts get anchored

In 1987, Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale gave estate agents a tour of a real house with a full information pack. The only thing that differed was the listed price. The agents' valuations moved with whatever list price they'd been shown, and most of them didn't mention the list price when asked what they'd based their valuation on.

If professionals can't shake off an anchor, the person across the table probably can't either.

## How to set a good anchor

1. **Know the [ZOPA](/guides/zopa-negotiation) first.** An anchor works best when it's ambitious but defensible. Too extreme, and the other side may walk away or stop taking you seriously.
2. **Give a reason.** "£52,000, based on what similar roles pay in London" is far stronger than "£52,000".
3. **Use a precise number.** A 2013 study by Malia Mason and colleagues at Columbia found that precise first offers drew smaller counteroffers than round ones, because they sound researched. £4,850 sounds calculated. £5,000 sounds like a guess.
4. **Go first when you know the value.** If you have good information, an early anchor helps you. If you don't, let them go first. There's more on this in our guide to the [first offer](/guides/first-offer-in-negotiation).

## How to defuse an anchor

When the other side opens with an extreme number, the worst response is to counter from it. Splitting the difference from a crazy anchor still leaves you far from where you should be.

Instead:

- **Name it.** "That's well outside what we've seen for this kind of work."
- **Don't counter immediately.** Ask how they got there. Weak anchors fall apart under questions.
- **Re-anchor.** Put your own reasoned number on the table and talk about that one.
- **Move to objective criteria.** Market data, comparable deals and published prices take the heat out of a number war.

## The takeaway

The first number sets the frame for everything that follows. Prepare it, justify it, and if someone else gets there first with something unreasonable, don't let it become the reference point.
