Supplier negotiations aren't one-off haggles. You'll deal with the same suppliers again and again, so the goal is a good deal *and* a relationship that keeps delivering. Here's how to get both.

## 1. Know what you're really buying

Price per unit is only part of the cost. Before you negotiate, work out the **total cost**: delivery, minimum order sizes, payment terms, defects and returns, switching costs and the time your team spends managing the supplier. A cheaper supplier with late deliveries can cost more.

## 2. Create competitive tension

Your strongest lever is a credible alternative, your [BATNA](/guides/what-is-batna). For significant purchases:

- get at least two or three quotes
- tell suppliers they're being compared, without bluffing about details
- consider running a short, structured tender for bigger contracts

Even if you plan to stay with your current supplier, competing quotes tell you what the market pays.

## 3. Understand the supplier's side

Ask what matters to them. Suppliers often care about:

- **Volume and predictability**, so they can plan production
- **Cash flow**, which makes faster payment valuable
- **Longer contracts**, which reduce their sales costs
- **Reference value**, if you're a well-known customer

These are things you can trade.

## 4. Trade terms, don't just squeeze price

Integrative moves usually beat a straight squeeze. For example:

> "If we commit to 12 months and pay in 14 days instead of 30, can you take 6% off the unit price?"

That's [logrolling](/guides/integrative-vs-distributive-negotiation): giving something cheap for you in return for something valuable.

## 5. Put the important things in the contract

Agree in writing on:

- price and how it can change (annual reviews, index links)
- delivery times and what happens if they're missed
- quality standards and returns
- payment terms
- how either side can exit

## 6. Play the long game

Suppliers you treat fairly tend to prioritise you when stock is short or deadlines are tight. Robert Axelrod's work on [tit for tat](/guides/tit-for-tat-strategy) is a good model: start cooperative, respond promptly to problems, forgive once they're fixed, and be predictable.

## Common mistakes

- Negotiating only on unit price and ignoring total cost
- Bluffing about competing quotes
- Squeezing so hard the supplier cuts corners on quality
- Forgetting to agree what happens when things go wrong

Good supplier negotiations look less like a fight and more like a well-run partnership with clear terms.
