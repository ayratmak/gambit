Asking for more money feels awkward, so most people wait to be offered it. That's usually a mistake. Managers rarely raise pay unprompted, and budgets get set whether or not you've spoken up.

## Pick the right moment

Timing matters more than the words you use.

- **Before budgets are fixed.** If pay is decided at an annual review, the decision is often made weeks before. Ask a few months ahead.
- **After a clear win.** A finished project, a new client or a problem solved gives you something concrete to point to.
- **Not in a crisis.** If the company has just announced cuts, wait or focus on non-pay benefits.

## Build a case about value, not need

Your rising rent or bills are real, but they're your problem, not your employer's. Build your case on three things:

1. **Results.** What have you delivered? Use numbers where you can: money saved, revenue won, hours freed up.
2. **Scope.** Has your job grown since your pay was set? New responsibilities are one of the strongest arguments.
3. **Market rate.** What do similar roles pay elsewhere? Job adverts with salaries and published surveys help.

## A script you can adapt

Book a meeting with an agenda, so it isn't an ambush.

> "I'd like to talk about my pay. Over the past year I've taken on the supplier contracts and cut our onboarding time by a third. Roles with this scope are paying around £52,000, and I'm on £46,000. I'd like to get to £51,500. What would it take?"

That last question matters. It turns a demand into a joint problem.

## Know your alternative

Your [BATNA](/guides/what-is-batna), what you'll do if the answer is no, quietly shapes how the conversation goes. If you'd happily stay without a rise, you can be relaxed. If you're interviewing elsewhere, you'll feel more confident, but only mention another offer if it's real and you'd genuinely take it.

## If the answer is no

A no today isn't the end. Ask:

- "What would need to be true for this to be a yes?"
- "Can we agree goals now and review in three months?"
- "If base pay is fixed, is there flexibility on a bonus, title, training or extra holiday?"

Then write up what you agreed and send it to your manager. A follow-up email turns a vague promise into a commitment.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Threatening to quit** unless you mean it.
- **Comparing yourself to named colleagues.** It makes your manager defensive.
- **Accepting the first no** without asking what would change it.
- **Asking by email out of the blue.** Have the conversation first, then confirm in writing.

Asking is uncomfortable for ten minutes. Not asking can cost you for years.
