Two cars drive straight at each other. Whoever swerves is the "chicken". If neither swerves, they crash. That's the setup of one of game theory's most important games, and it explains strikes, standoffs and brinkmanship.

## The payoffs

- **You swerve, they don't:** you lose face, they win.
- **They swerve, you don't:** you win.
- **Both swerve:** a draw, no harm done.
- **Neither swerves:** both crash, the worst outcome by far.

Each player wants the other to swerve. And unlike the [prisoner's dilemma](/guides/prisoners-dilemma-examples), there's no option that's best whatever the other does. If they're going to hold on, you should swerve. If they're going to swerve, you should hold on.

## How to win: commitment

The strategist Herman Kahn had a famous answer. Remove your own ability to swerve, and make sure the other driver sees it. Throw your steering wheel out of the window. Now the only person who can prevent the crash is them.

This is the paradox at the heart of chicken: **you gain power by reducing your options.** Thomas Schelling, who later shared the 2005 Nobel prize in economics, built much of his work on this idea of credible commitment.

## Chicken in real life

- **Strikes.** A union that votes to strike before talks begin has committed in public, which raises the pressure on the employer to give way.
- **Budget standoffs.** Governments and parliaments sometimes head for a shutdown deadline with each side waiting for the other to blink.
- **Price wars.** Each company hopes the other will give up first.
- **The Cuban missile crisis, 1962.** The US and Soviet Union edged towards confrontation, each hoping the other would back down, before a negotiated settlement.

## Why it's dangerous

Commitment only works if the other side sees it and believes it. If both sides commit, or one misreads the other, the crash happens. Many real-world disasters, from failed mergers to long strikes, are games of chicken where nobody blinked.

## Using the idea safely

1. **Commit to what you'll accept, not to a fight.** "We can't go above £50,000; it's board policy" is a commitment without a threat.
2. **Make it visible and credible.** Hidden commitments don't change anyone's behaviour.
3. **Leave the other side a way to back down with dignity.** People will crash rather than be humiliated.
4. **Know your [walk-away point](/guides/when-to-walk-away)** before you start, so you never commit to something you can't afford to lose.

Would you throw the wheel? The honest answer for most negotiations is: only if you're sure they can see it, and only if you can live with the crash.
