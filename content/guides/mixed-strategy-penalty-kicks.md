If a penalty taker always shoots left, the keeper learns to dive left. If a keeper always dives right, takers learn to shoot left. **The only way not to be exploited is to be unpredictable.** Game theorists call this a **mixed strategy**.

## What a mixed strategy is

Instead of always picking the same option, you pick at random, with set probabilities. A penalty taker might shoot to their natural side 60% of the time and the other side 40%, in no pattern a keeper can read.

The right mix makes your opponent indifferent: whichever way the keeper dives, they save the same share of shots. At that point, there's nothing for them to exploit. It's a [Nash equilibrium](/guides/nash-equilibrium-explained) in mixed strategies.

## What the research found

The economist Ignacio Palacios-Huerta studied over a thousand professional penalties and found that players' choices matched mixed-strategy predictions remarkably closely. Takers and keepers were, in effect, playing game theory without knowing it.

Other researchers looked at goalkeepers. A 2007 study led by Michael Bar-Eli found that keepers dived left or right on about 94% of penalties, even though staying in the centre would have saved a share of the shots aimed down the middle. Their explanation: missing a save after diving feels better, and looks better to fans, than missing while standing still. It's called action bias.

## Mixed strategies elsewhere

- **Tennis serves.** Servers mix wide and body serves so returners can't anticipate.
- **Poker.** Players bluff some of the time, so opponents can't read their bets.
- **Security.** Police patrols and ticket inspections are randomised so nobody can predict when they'll be checked.
- **Tax audits.** Random checks keep everyone honest at lower cost.
- **Promotions.** Shops that discount at unpredictable times stop customers simply waiting for the sale.

## In negotiation

Being completely predictable can cost you. If you always split the difference, or always accept the second offer, experienced counterparts will learn your pattern. Vary your concessions, sometimes hold firm, and don't let your behaviour become a script someone can read.

The lesson from the penalty spot: **when your opponent is trying to guess your move, the best move is one they can't guess.**
